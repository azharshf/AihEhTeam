PREVENTION AND LIFESTYLE RECOMMENDATIONS FOR DYSLIPIDEMIA

WHY PREVENTION MATTERS:
Untreated dyslipidemia can lead to heart attack, stroke, coronary artery disease, peripheral artery disease, and pancreatitis (especially with very high triglycerides). Prevention through lifestyle changes is often the first line of defense.

DIETARY RECOMMENDATIONS:
- Reduce saturated fat intake (less than 7% of total calories). Avoid fatty meats, full-fat dairy, palm oil, and coconut oil.
- Eliminate trans fats. Avoid partially hydrogenated oils, margarine, and many fried foods.
- Increase soluble fiber intake. Eat oats, barley, beans, lentils, fruits, and vegetables.
- Eat more omega-3 fatty acids. Include fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines), walnuts, and flaxseeds.
- Increase plant stanols and sterols. Found in fortified foods.
- Limit dietary cholesterol to less than 200 mg per day for high-risk individuals.
- Reduce refined carbohydrates and sugary drinks, which raise triglycerides.

MALAYSIAN-SPECIFIC FOOD SWAPS:
- Instead of nasi lemak with coconut milk: Try nasi with less santan or use reduced-fat coconut milk.
- Instead of deep-fried snacks (goreng pisang, cucur udang): Try steamed or baked versions.
- Instead of roti canai with ghee: Try chapati or wholemeal roti.
- Instead of teh tarik with condensed milk: Try teh-O or teh with low-fat milk.
- Instead of char kway teow (high in lard): Try kuey teow soup version.
- Choose more ulam (raw herbs), sayur (vegetables), and tempeh/tahu (soy protein).
- Use olive oil or canola oil instead of palm oil for cooking.

EXERCISE RECOMMENDATIONS:
- At least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity (brisk walking, cycling, swimming).
- Or 75 minutes per week of vigorous activity (running, aerobic classes).
- Include resistance/strength training 2 or more days per week.
- Reduce sedentary time. Stand or walk every 30 minutes.

OTHER LIFESTYLE CHANGES:
- Quit smoking. Smoking lowers HDL-C and damages blood vessels.
- Limit alcohol intake. Excessive alcohol raises triglycerides.
- Manage stress. Chronic stress can raise cholesterol levels.
- Improve sleep quality. Poor sleep is linked to higher cholesterol.
- Maintain a healthy weight. Losing 5-10% of body weight can improve lipid levels.

WHEN TO SEE A DOCTOR:
- If LDL-C is 190 mg/dL or above.
- If triglycerides are 500 mg/dL or above.
- If you have diabetes, CKD, or HIV and are aged 40-75.
- If you have a family history of early heart disease.
- If lifestyle changes alone are not enough after 3-6 months.
- If you experience any emergency warning symptoms.

STRESS AND DYSLIPIDEMIA:
Chronic stress can contribute to dyslipidemia through increased cortisol, stress eating of unhealthy foods, reduced physical activity, poor sleep, and increased smoking or alcohol use. Stress management techniques include regular exercise, mindfulness, adequate sleep, social support, and professional counseling if needed.
