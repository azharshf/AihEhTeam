WHAT IS DYSLIPIDEMIA?

Dyslipidemia is a medical condition where the fat levels in a person's blood are abnormal. These fats include low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides, and total cholesterol.

It does not simply mean that there is fat in the blood. Everyone has some fat in the blood because the body needs cholesterol and triglycerides for normal functions such as hormone production, digestion, cell structure, and energy storage. Dyslipidemia means these levels are unhealthy, such as too much LDL-C, too much triglyceride, too much total cholesterol, or too little HDL-C.

The main lipid components are:
- LDL-C: Often called bad cholesterol. High LDL-C can contribute to plaque buildup inside blood vessels, increasing the risk of heart attack and stroke.
- HDL-C: Often called good cholesterol. HDL-C helps carry cholesterol away from blood vessels and back to the liver.
- Triglycerides: A type of blood fat used for energy. High levels can increase heart risk, and very high levels can increase the risk of pancreatitis.
- Total cholesterol: The overall cholesterol measurement in the blood.

Dyslipidemia is often called a silent condition because most people do not feel sick. Many people only discover it through a lipid profile blood test. If untreated, dyslipidemia can increase the risk of serious diseases such as heart attack, stroke, blocked blood vessels, peripheral artery disease, fatty liver disease, and pancreatitis.
